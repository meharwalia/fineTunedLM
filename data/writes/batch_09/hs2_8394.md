In "The Second Sex", Simone de Beauvoir describes the perspective of a woman in society as one that men always define. Cultural, social, and political constraints limit her freedom and autonomy.

De Beauvoir argues that women are not born women. They become women through socialization. Socialization teaches them to see themselves as inferior to men. It also teaches them to accept their subordination as natural and inevitable. The family, education, religion, and the media work together in this process. They reinforce the idea that women are secondary to men and that their primary role is to serve and support men.

She argues that the second-class status of women is not inherent to their biology. Historical and cultural forces produce it. Society has denied women access to education, political power, and economic independence. Legal and social restrictions have limited their freedom and autonomy.

The book also explores other issues of women's oppression:

- Society objectifies and sexualizes women.
- Motherhood affects women's lives.
- Society denies women access to intellectual and cultural pursuits.

De Beauvoir argues that women must reject the idea that they are naturally inferior to men. They must work to overcome the cultural, social, and political barriers that limit their freedom and autonomy.

"The Second Sex" is a seminal work of feminist theory. It critiques the cultural, social, and political forces that limit women's freedom.
