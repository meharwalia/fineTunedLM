The level of infection in animal populations changes with the virus, its transmission dynamics and the human-animal interaction. Before a virus leaps to humans, it typically passes through an intermediate animal host population. There, it is widespread.

SARS is an example. The intermediate host was the Asian palm civet. The animal reservoir was the Malaysian Horseshoe bat.

The virus was widespread among Asian palm civets. Up to 70% of civets in markets tested positive for it.1

Ebola is another example. The disease probably originated from infected fruit bats. The virus then spread through the intermediate host population of primates, such as gorillas and chimpanzees.

Ebola virus can be found in the blood of fruit bats. Up to 78% of fruit bats in areas carried the virus.2

The leap to humans typically needs a mutation in the virus. The mutation lets the virus transmit easily from an animal to a human.

After the mutation, the virus can spread fast among humans. This is most likely where people are densely packed and in close contact.
