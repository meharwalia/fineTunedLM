The Bill of Rights came from efforts to secure the ratification of the United States Constitution.

The Constitution did not include a bill of rights when it was first proposed in 1787. This worried anti-federalists. They feared that the new federal government would trample on the individual liberties of citizens.

Federalists supported the Constitution. In response to these concerns, they agreed to add a bill of rights as a condition for ratification. James Madison was a federalist and a member of the House of Representatives.

He drafted the bill of rights. He proposed amendments to the Constitution that protect individual liberties. These include freedom of speech, religion and the press.

They also include the right to a fair trial and to bear arms.

Congress debated and revised the proposed amendments. Ten of them were adopted and ratified by the necessary three-fourths of the states. These first 10 amendments are the Bill of Rights.

The states ratified them on December 15, 1791. They protect individual rights and freedoms:

- freedom of speech, religion, the press and assembly
- the right to bear arms
- protection against unreasonable searches and seizures
- the right to a fair and speedy trial
- the prohibition of cruel and unusual punishment

The Bill of Rights gave explicit protections for individual liberties. It also addressed concerns about the potential for government overreach.

It remains a cornerstone of the American system of government. It continues to protect the rights and freedoms of American citizens.
