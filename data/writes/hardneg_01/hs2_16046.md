**Solubility of Oxides in Water**

- It should be noted that oxides are generally insoluble in water due to the chemical bonds that are formed and the molecular structure that is subsequently produced, and essentially every oxide consists of oxygen combined with another element.
- When the elements are metals, ionic bonds are often formed by the oxides, and electrons are transferred from the metal to the oxygen, which results in a compound that is strong and is not easily dissociated in water.
- The solubility of a compound is determined by its ability to interact with water molecules, and it is important to note that a compound must interact with the partial charges of the polar water molecule through ion-dipole interaction or hydrogen bonding in order to dissolve.
- Metal oxides mostly fail to form these interactions, since their ions are tightly bound in a solid lattice, which makes them insoluble in various circumstances.
- Some oxides, such as those of the alkali metals (for example, sodium oxide), are utilised in water because stronger interactions are formed with water molecules, and the ions are subsequently surrounded by water molecules and dissolved.
- Oxides of non-metals can also exhibit covalent bonding, which contributes to their insolubility, and these variations in bonding affect how oxides interact with water.
