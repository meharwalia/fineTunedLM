The percentage of ferrite in a low alloy steel after cooling depends on the cooling rate, the composition of the steel and the temperature at which the steel is quenched.

A higher cooling rate typically gives a lower percentage of ferrite. A faster cooling rate reduces the time for the transformation from austenite to ferrite. This transformation is slow and diffusion-controlled.

The relationship between the cooling rate and the percentage of ferrite is not straightforward. The composition of the steel and the quenching temperature also influence it.

Empirical formulas that relate the cooling rate to the percentage of ferrite in low alloy steel exist. They are typically specific to one steel composition and quenching process. Examples:

- The Johnson-Mehl-Avrami (JMA) equation: This mathematical model describes the transformation of austenite to ferrite during cooling. It predicts the percentage of ferrite as a function of the cooling rate and the steel composition.
- The Arrhenius equation: This mathematical model describes the effect of temperature and cooling rate on the transformation of austenite to ferrite. It predicts the percentage of ferrite as a function of the cooling rate, the steel composition and the quenching temperature.
- The Schaeffler diagram: This graph shows the relationship between the cooling rate, the quenching temperature and the percentage of ferrite. It predicts the percentage of ferrite from the cooling rate and the quenching temperature for one steel composition.

The percentage of ferrite depends on a complex interplay of the cooling rate, the steel composition and the quenching temperature. To predict it accurately for one steel, you typically need experimental testing and analysis.
