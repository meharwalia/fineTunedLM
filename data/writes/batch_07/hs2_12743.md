Developing cancer drugs is a complex, challenging process with 6 steps.

1. Identify a target: Scientists identify a specific protein or pathway in the growth and survival of cancer cells. This target is often different from the proteins and pathways in normal cells.
2. Develop a drug: Scientists develop a drug that inhibits the target protein or pathway. They test different compounds to find one that is effective and specific to the target.
3. Preclinical testing: Scientists test the drug in laboratory studies with cancer cell lines and animal models. The goal is to find the safety and effectiveness of the drug. The tests also identify potential side effects.
4. Clinical trials: After the drug passes preclinical testing, scientists test it in clinical trials. These trials test the drug in humans. They find its safety, side effects and effectiveness.
5. FDA approval: If the clinical trials succeed, scientists submit the drug to the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for approval. The FDA reviews the data from the clinical trials. It decides if the drug is safe and effective to treat cancer.
6. Post-approval studies: After approval, scientists continue to study the drug. They learn more about its side effects, interactions with other drugs and long-term effects.

A new cancer drug typically takes years and millions of dollars to develop. Only a small percentage of drugs that enter clinical trials are approved to treat cancer.
