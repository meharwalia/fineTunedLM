Oxides are generally insoluble in water because of their chemical bonds and structure. An oxide consists of oxygen and one other element. When the other element is a metal, the oxide often forms ionic bonds.

Electrons move from the metal to the oxygen. The result is a strong compound that does not easily dissociate in water.

A compound dissolves in water only if it interacts with water molecules. Water is polar. It has a partial positive charge on one side and a partial negative charge on the other.

A compound must interact with these charges through ion-dipole interaction or hydrogen bonding. Metal oxides mostly do not form these interactions easily. Their ions are tightly bound in a solid lattice, so they are insoluble.

Oxides of the alkali metals, such as sodium oxide, form stronger interactions with water molecules. They dissociate into ions. Water molecules surround the ions, and the oxide dissolves.

Oxides of non-metals have covalent bonding, which also contributes to their insolubility. These variations in bonding affect how oxides interact with water.
